      SYSophia Yazzourh

      From Q-learning to Near-Equivalent Policies: Expanding Treatment Options in Precision Medicine

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Precision medicine aims to tailor therapeutic decisions to individual patient characteristics and evolving clinical information. Dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs) provide a framework for developing sequential treatment strategies, with the objective of optimizing patient outcomes. Among the statistical methods used to estimate DTRs, Q-learning is a regression-based approach that relies on backward recursion to identify optimal treatment decisions at successive stages.

      In this presentation, we first introduce the fundamental principles of Q-learning, highlighting how sequential decision rules are estimated from retrospective data. We then discuss a limitation of the conventional framework: its focus on identifying a single optimal treatment policy, even when several treatment options may yield very similar expected outcomes. To address this limitation, we introduce Near-Equivalent Q-learning (NEQ), an extension that constructs families of treatment policies whose expected values remain within a specified tolerance of the optimum. The proposed approach relies on two user-specified hyperparameters: a tolerance parameter, ε, controlling the allowable departure from the optimal expected value, and a selection stage, tε, determining when the tolerance criterion is applied during backward recursion.

      We illustrate the proposed framework through three settings: a single-stage treatment decision problem highlighting regions of treatment indifference, a multi-stage simulated oncology model examining the influence of the two hyperparameters, and an application to the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial investigating near-equivalent antihypertensive treatment recommendations. Together, these examples demonstrate how extending Q-learning beyond a single optimal policy can provide greater flexibility in sequential treatment decision-making.

      Speaker bio

      Sophia Yazzourh is a postdoctoral researcher at McGill University. She develops statistical methods for choosing sequences of treatments in precision medicine.

      LPLuis Martín Peña

      Computational identification of spatial niches in cancer

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is an aggressive cancer with poor prognosis and marked cellular heterogeneity. This heterogeneity makes tumor classification difficult, despite the importance of identifying clinically and biologically meaningful tumor states for therapeutic interpretation. Transcriptomic data provide a natural framework to study this problem at different scales. Single-cell RNA sequencing gives access to the transcriptional state of individual cells, while spatial transcriptomics retains information about their organization within the tissue. Together, these technologies make it possible to investigate the broader cellular environments in which these states occur. Our work focuses on the inference of tumor ecosystems from such transcriptomic data: identifying reproducible cellular and transcriptional configurations that can be defined, compared, and ultimately classified. This inference problem sits in a methodological landscape that includes heterogeneous datasets, approaches, and a wide range of analysis strategies, none of which is specifically designed to benchmark ecosystem inference as a task in itself. We propose to formulate this question as a dedicated data challenge. The objective is to define explicit prediction tasks, datasets, simulations, and evaluation criteria in order to compare methods under controlled conditions, and to assess how reliably tumor ecosystems can be inferred from transcriptomic data.

      Speaker bio

      Luis Martín Peña is a doctoral researcher in bioinformatics at Université Grenoble Alpes and the Laboratoire d’informatique de Grenoble. He studies pancreatic tumour heterogeneity using spatial transcriptomics.

      DeepStress: Stress-Testing Deep Search Agents

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      While search agents demonstrate impressive capabilities in multi-step question answering, their robustness to poor-quality evidence remains under-explored. This phenomenon occurs rarely in realistic benchmarks but can lead to dramatic failure in real life applications. I will present in this talk "DeepStress", a stress testing framework that controls the frequency of challenging evidence by replacing the retrieval module of search agents with a controlled synthetic environment.       IBImen Bajar

      Beneath the Face: Identity in Brain MRI

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Brain MRI carries highly sensitive personal information, not only in facial features but in the unique anatomical structure of each brain, often called a “brain fingerprint.” This talk presents two studies that ask whether this identifying information remains encoded after the scan is transformed, first by defacing, then by pretrained encoders. We first evaluate defacing. Across four state-of-the-art methods (fsl_deface, PyDeface, PyFaceWipe, mri_reface) applied to T1-weighted scans from OASIS-2, a similarity-based attack using probabilistic linear discriminant analysis over FreeSurfer neuroanatomical features achieves rank-1 patient linkage with success rates of 66–73%. This shows that removing the face does not remove identity. We then ask whether sharing learned representations instead of raw scans resolves the problem. Evaluating five frozen pretrained encoders spanning different architectures, pretraining objectives and training domains on OASIS-2 and MIRIAD, under an attacker with access only to released embeddings, the corresponding public encoder, and auxiliary scans, rank-1 patient linkage reaches up to 99% on MIRIAD and exceeds 90% on OASIS-2, despite the absence of any identification objective during pretraining. The same embeddings also support recorded-sex prediction at 90.9% and 95.5% balanced accuracy. Identity is encoded in brain MRI and it survives both defacing and encoding into learned representations. New anonymization methods are needed.

      Speaker bio

      Imen Bajar is a doctoral researcher at INSA Lyon and ÉTS Montréal. She investigates whether people can be reidentified from brain MRI scans and their learned representations.

      IZImrane Zaakour

      The Pareto Frontier of Randomized Learning-Augmented Online Bidding

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Online algorithms make sequential decisions without knowing the future. Classical worst-case analysis provides strong guarantees against uncertainty, but can be overly pessimistic when useful information about future instances is available. Learning-augmented algorithms address this limitation by incorporating predictions, typically obtained from historical data or machine-learning models, while retaining worst-case guarantees when these predictions are inaccurate.

      In this talk, I will introduce this framework through the online bidding problem, a fundamental model in which an algorithm must progressively allocate resources without knowing the amount ultimately required. I will first discuss the classical deterministic and randomized solutions and then show how access to a prediction changes the problem. The central question becomes how much an algorithm can benefit from an accurate prediction while remaining protected against arbitrarily inaccurate ones.

      This tension is captured by the trade-off between consistency, which measures performance when the prediction is correct, and robustness, which controls worst-case performance. The talk will present new results characterizing the Pareto frontier between these two objectives for randomized online bidding, including a regime in which matching upper and lower bounds provide an exact characterization. The results illustrate more broadly how randomization and predictions can be combined to move beyond classical worst-case analysis without abandoning rigorous performance guarantees.

      Speaker bio

      Imrane Zaakour is a doctoral student at CentraleSupélec, affiliated with ILLS and the FairPlay team. He studies randomized algorithms for making sequential decisions with imperfect predictions.

      VGVincent Gripon

      D5P4: Partition Determinantal Point Process for Diversity in Parallel Discrete Diffusion Decoding

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Discrete diffusion models are promising alternatives to autoregressive approaches for text generation, yet their decoding methods remain under-studied. Standard autoregressive search procedures, such as beam search, do not directly apply to iterative denoising, where hypotheses are complete intermediate sequences rather than left-to-right prefixes. Furthermore, existing diffusion decoding procedures only provide limited control over the diversity and coverage of retained hypotheses.

      In this work, we introduce D5P4, a beam-style decoding method tailored to discrete diffusion models, which casts intermediate beam selection as MAP inference under a partitioned Determinantal Point Process. This yields a model-internal batch objective that balances quality and diversity without external verifiers. Experiments on open-ended generation, question answering, and mathematical reasoning show that D5P4 improves diversity and pass@k coverage while matching or surpassing baseline quality and fidelity. D5P4 also improves decoding efficiency, reducing post-forward latency and peak memory while scaling to large candidate pools.

      Speaker bio

      Vincent Gripon is a professor at IMT Atlantique and a member of Lab-STICC. His research interests include neural network compression, learning from few examples and foundation models for EEG.

      Remote talk with a group viewing at ILLS

      JKJung-hun Kim

      I.i.d. Prophet Inequalities with Discounted Rewards: As Hard as the Non-i.i.d. Case

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      We study prophet inequalities with discounted rewards, where i.i.d. base rewards are multiplicatively discounted over time. Our main message is that even this structured and arbitrarily weak form of nonstationarity can erase the classical advantage of the stationary i.i.d. setting. Focusing on single-quantile threshold policies, we show that the competitive ratio transitions from the classical 1-1/e guarantee to a fundamental 1/2 barrier as discounting accumulates over many phases in a canonical regime with a common-decay factor and equal-length phases. We further show that, in the same regime, the 1/2 barrier persists even for arbitrary stopping rules. Consequently, i.i.d. base rewards under discounting can be as hard as the fully non-i.i.d. case. On the algorithmic side, we design single-quantile threshold rules that attain the tight bounds by calibrating acceptance decisions to an effective horizon induced by discounting, and we extend this calibration to heterogeneous decay factors and unequal phase lengths. We further show that a similar discontinuous breakdown persists in an infinite-horizon continuous-decay benchmark, where arbitrarily weak decay collapses the stationary benchmark from 1 to 1/2.

      Speaker bio

      Jung-hun Kim is a postdoctoral researcher on the FairPlay team at CREST, ENSAE Paris. He studies optimal stopping and the guarantees achievable when decisions must be made sequentially.

      ASAmin Sghaier

      Learning Autoregressive Image Generation Orders via Variational Inference

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      What's the right order to generate an image? Autoregressive image models default to a raster order (left to right), which is convenient but, unlike text's natural reading order, isn't an obvious choice. We set out to learn a better order using a variational inference approach: an order encoder receives the full image as input and proposes the order in which a causal decoder should generate the tokens. While the same framework can discover good orders in other domains, it wasn't helpful in ours (class-conditional image generation). We trace this back to an information mismatch between the encoder and the decoder, and identify the conditions under which variational order learning can be expected to work.

    • 23 April 2026 · 14:00

      FGFrédéric Gosselin

      Vision and Mental Imagery

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      A central question in cognitive neuroscience is whether imagery and visual perception rely on the same underlying representations and mechanisms. A common view is that the mental images we generate internally closely resemble the representations engaged during actual vision. If this is true, one would expect strong links between the quality of mental imagery and perceptual performance. However, our recent work suggests that this relationship is more complex than traditionally assumed. Across several studies, we found limited evidence that subjective vividness of mental images is systematically related to visual recognition or discrimination abilities. Even when the tasks were designed to maximize overlap between what participants imagined and what they later perceived, the association remained weak. These findings suggest that subjective vividness may not be a straightforward index of the representational quality shared between imagery and perception. This led us to explore vividness more directly. To better understand what drives vividness judgments, we developed a large-scale database of vividness ratings for natural scenes. By collecting a very large number of ratings across a broad image set, this work aims to identify which visual characteristics make certain images easier to imagine vividly than others. In parallel, computational modeling approaches are being used to predict vividness from image features, opening the possibility of isolating the visual properties that support strong mental imagery. At the same time, we are developing alternatives to subjective vividness ratings as a way to compare imagery and vision more directly at the representational level. One promising approach uses behavioral similarity spaces: instead of asking participants how vivid an image feels, we compare the structure of relationships among imagined and perceived stimuli. This work provides evidence that the representational geometry of imagery aligns substantially with that of vision, beyond what can be explained by semantic descriptions alone. In other words, imagery and perception may share internal structure even when simple vividness judgments fail to capture it. A complementary line of work investigates shared mechanisms using psychophysical tools such as visual adaptation. Here, the question is whether imagining a stimulus can bias subsequent perception in the same way that actual visual exposure does. Early evidence suggests that imagined stimuli can indeed produce measurable aftereffects, even at relatively high levels of visual processing, offering a powerful tool to probe the overlap between imagery and perception. Taken together, these projects suggest a nuanced picture: mental imagery and vision do appear to share representational and mechanistic components, but this overlap is not always reflected in standard vividness ratings. Moving beyond subjective reports toward representational and psychophysical measures may therefore provide a more precise understanding of how the mind’s eye relates to vision itself.

      Speaker bio

      Frédéric Gosselin is a professor of psychology at Université de Montréal. He investigates the mechanisms that visual perception and mental imagery have in common.

      ANAlasdair Newson

      Generative Models and Deep Priors for Image and Video Restoration and Editing

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Modern algorithms for image and video restoration and editing now rely on the impressive capabilities of powerful neural networks, in particular generative models. By generating examples of image data, we are training a neural network to implicitly discover the space of realistic images, and this knowledge, often referred to as a "Deep Prior", can subsequently be employed for the purposes of restoration and editing. In this talk, I will present three topics which employ such an approach.

      The first work focuses on video inpainting, that is, filling in missing or damaged regions in videos. We propose an approach based on diffusion models, a family of particularly powerful generative models that rely on the progressive inversion of a noising process. However, the large size of these models, in terms of the number of parameters, makes them difficult to use, especially for high-dimensional data such as videos. Therefore, in this work, we propose a "frugal" approach to video inpainting, based on the assumption of self-similarity in videos, i.e., the presence of redundant content. We obtain results that are equivalent or superior to state-of-the-art models, while using much more compact models, by one or more orders of magnitude.

      In the second work, we use the projected gradient descent algorithm to solve inverse problems, such as super-resolution and image inpainting, replacing the projection step with a deep neural network (the deep prior). In particular, we introduce a Stochastic Orthogonal Regularization ("SOR") of the network, which allows control over the convergence rate of the projected gradient descent algorithm, and empirically improves the quality of the solutions. I will show quantitative and qualitative experiments which illustrate this, particularly when the degradation operator is more severe.

      Finally, I will present a series of works which employ generative models for image editing, with the goal of modifying semantic content such as the expression of a face in images. These methods structure the latent spaces of generative models to correspond to the desired semantic attributed, enabling simple and controllable editing.

      Speaker bio

      Alasdair Newson is an associate professor at Télécom Paris. He develops computer vision and generative modelling methods for analysing, restoring and editing images and video.

      Adversarial Perspectives on Local Differential Privacy: Attacks, Auditing, and Protocol Design

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Local Differential Privacy (LDP) has emerged as a practical paradigm for privacy-preserving data analytics, enabling strong guarantees without relying on a trusted server. However, despite its formal definitions, recent research has shown that LDP mechanisms are vulnerable to adversarial inference, with attacks capable of reconstructing or inferring user data even under ε-LDP guarantees. This talk presents an adversarial perspective on LDP, revealing both its weaknesses and opportunities. The talk first examines how reconstruction and inference attacks expose vulnerabilities that remain hidden when considering only the theoretical parameter ε. These very attacks can then be repurposed as auditing tools, allowing the statistical estimation of the effective privacy loss of deployed protocols and revealing discrepancies between theoretical guarantees and real-world leakage. Building on these insights, we will introduce a framework for refining and designing new LDP protocols through multi-objective optimization, balancing privacy, utility, and robustness to attacks. The resulting protocols achieve more favorable trade-offs, substantially reducing adversarial success rates while maintaining practical accuracy. Overall, the talk bridges the gap between theory and practice, providing concrete strategies for designing attack-aware, auditable, and adaptive LDP mechanisms.

      Speaker bio

      QBQuentin Bertrand

      Toward Understanding the Generalization of Flow Matching

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Modern deep generative models can now produce high-quality synthetic samples that are often indistinguishable from real training data. A growing body of research aims to understand why recent methods, such as diffusion and flow matching techniques, generalize so effectively. Among the proposed explanations are the inductive biases of deep learning architectures and the stochastic nature of the conditional flow matching loss. In this work, we rule out the noisy nature of the loss as a key factor driving generalization in flow matching. First, we empirically show that in high-dimensional settings, the stochastic and closed-form versions of the flow matching loss yield nearly equivalent losses. Then, using state-of-the-art flow matching models on standard image datasets, we demonstrate that both variants achieve comparable statistical performance, with the surprising observation that using the closed-form can even improve performance.

      Speaker bio

      Quentin Bertrand is a researcher at Inria in Lyon. He studies how deep generative models learn from data and how they generalize beyond their training examples.

      MLMarc Lafon

      Learning Vision-Language Uncertainties for Failure Prediction

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Reliable Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) and failure prediction remain open challenges for Vision-Language Models (VLMs). We introduce ViLU, a new vision-language uncertainty quantification framework that contextualizes uncertainty estimates by leveraging all task-relevant textual representations.

      ViLU builds an uncertainty-aware multi-modal representation by integrating the visual embedding, the predicted textual embedding, and an image-conditioned textual representation via cross-attention.

      LGLeonhard Grosse

      Pointwise Maximal Leakage - Privacy Guarantees and Mechanisms towards Modern Applications

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Modern data processing methods increasingly subject individuals' sensitive personal data to the risk of malicious inference by big data service providers or third parties. To mitigate this risk, provable privacy guarantees for data processing systems have emerged as a promising approach. These guarantees are based on an underlying privacy measure that aims to efficiently and precisely measure a notion of privacy that should be aligned with legal definitions. In recent years, differential privacy (DP) has become the de facto standard measure used for providing stringent guarantees. However, it can be argued that DP guarantees cannot rule out the possibility of privacy violations for databases with correlated entries. Further, the strict definition of DP based on neighboring databases poses a challenge in learning systems, as useful privacy guarantees with DP often severely diminish model performance. These shortcomings motivate the development of measures that quantify privacy in a Bayesian framework. In this talk, we will introduce a recently proposed Bayesian measure called pointwise maximal leakage (PML). PML is based on two equivalent explicit threat models, and enables clear inferential guarantees that can rule out the possibility of general inference attacks. We develop the measure from ground up, and show how it is obtained from the underlying threat models. Further, we will outline how the inferential nature of the measure can be used to quantitatively interpret the privacy parameter in PML guarantees. Finally, we will show how the measure can be used to design discrete data sanitization mechanisms that improve data utility compared to DP.

      Speaker bio

      Leonhard Grosse studies information privacy at KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm. His work uses models of what an observer can infer about an individual to design privacy mechanisms and quantify their guarantees.

      LFLouis Fournier

      Cyclic Data Parallelism for Efficient Parallelism of Deep Neural Networks

      Abstract and speaker bio
      Abstract

      Training large deep learning models requires parallelization techniques to scale. In existing methods such as Data Parallelism or ZeRO-DP, micro-batches of data are processed in parallel, which creates two drawbacks: the total memory required to store the model's activations peaks at the end of the forward pass, and gradients must be simultaneously averaged at the end of the backpropagation step. We propose Cyclic Data Parallelism, a novel paradigm shifting the execution of the micro-batches from simultaneous to sequential, with a uniform delay. At the cost of a slight gradient delay, the total memory taken by activations is constant, and the gradient communications are balanced during the training step. With Model Parallelism, our technique reduces the number of GPUs needed, by sharing GPUs across micro-batches. Within the ZeRO-DP framework, our technique allows communication of the model states with point-to-point operations rather than a collective broadcast operation. We illustrate the strength of our approach on the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet datasets.

      Speaker bio

      At the time of the talk, Louis Fournier was a doctoral student at ISIR, Sorbonne Université. He studied how to distribute neural network training across GPUs while controlling memory use and communication costs.
